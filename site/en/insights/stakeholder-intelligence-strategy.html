Why stakeholder intelligence should sit closer to strategy.

Communication teams often see signals early. The question is whether those signals are interpreted before the decision is made.

In most organisations, the communication team is one of the first places where signals arrive. A journalist asks an unexpected question. A community leader raises the same concern for the third time. A regulator changes tone in a meeting. A partner stops replying as quickly as before.

Individually these look like operational details. Together they often describe a shift in how the organisation is perceived, and in what stakeholders expect from it.

The problem is that these signals are usually handled as communication tasks: answer the journalist, prepare a statement, schedule a meeting. They rarely reach the table where strategic decisions are made, at least not in a structured way and not early enough.

Stakeholder intelligence is the discipline of collecting those signals, interpreting them and presenting them to leadership in a form that supports decisions. It is not a report of media coverage. It is an answer to a simple question: what is changing in our environment, and what does it mean for what we are about to do?

Three practical steps make a difference. First, agree which stakeholders and issues really matter for the next twelve months. Second, create a regular rhythm where signals from media, field teams and relationships are brought together and discussed. Third, connect that discussion to specific decisions, not to general awareness.

In complex operating environments such as Mozambique, where formal structures do not always show how influence works, this matters even more. The organisations that read their environment well are usually the ones that avoid surprises.
